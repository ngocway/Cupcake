# Rhythm and titles

How a lesson deck keeps attention: an emotional arc, deliberate changes of pace, and titles that say something. Adapted from pitch-deck practice (Nancy Duarte's sparkline, copywriting formulas) and filtered for teaching; the sales-only parts are listed at the end as things not to use.

## 1. The emotional arc of a lesson

A sales deck runs curiosity, frustration, hope, trust, action. A lesson runs a similar line, but the goal is that the learner **can do something**:

| Stretch | Feeling you want | Typical slides |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Curiosity, or a jolt of recognising a mistake you make | `title`, a try-it-first `question` |
| Objectives and agenda | Reassurance: I know where this goes | `objectives`, `agenda` |
| Each part | Mild discomfort with the old way, then understanding, then "I can do this" | `compare` (old and new), `cards`, `process`, `two-column`, `question` |
| Evidence | Trust, because there is proof | `big-numbers`, `chart`, always with a source |
| End of a part | Relief: one sentence to remember | `key-message` |
| Close | Wanting to act now | `summary`, `closing` with one concrete task and a deadline |

When you plan the deck, add a "feeling" column next to the "idea" column. Three or four slides in a row with the same feeling (all explanation) need a change of pace in between.

## 2. Break the rhythm at 1/3 and 2/3

Attention sags around a third and two thirds of the way through a talk, and a 45-minute lesson is no different. So:
- Around the 1/3 and 2/3 marks put a slide that looks **clearly different** from its neighbours: a `question` (the background changes colour), a `key-message` (one sentence in the middle), `big-numbers` or a `chart` (one number), an `image` (one real picture). The script warns when neither mark has one nearby.
- Alternate "how it is" and "how it could be": a slide that shows the mistake or the old way, then one that shows the result of doing it right (a `compare`, or two `image` slides, before and after). Never five theory slides in a row.
- A `section` slide is a short breather of 2 to 4 seconds, not a place to lecture.

## 3. Titles are statements

A label title ("Principle 2", "Data") teaches nothing. A statement title says the point of the slide, so a learner who only reads the titles still gets the lesson.

| Pattern | Teaching example |
|---|---|
| Stop [the mistake] | Stop reading your slides aloud |
| [Number] jobs in [time] | Three jobs in thirty seconds |
| [Old way] loses [X], [new way] keeps it | Talking faster loses the learner, cutting keeps the goal |
| Why [something happens] | Why videos must stay short |
| [Who] [does what] and [measurable result] | Learners who act remember more |
| From [pain] to [result] | From slide deck to first video |

Keep titles under two lines at 36 pt (in capitals they run about 15% wider), no full stop at the end, and no tidy three-adjective lists (see `plain-human-voice.md`). Avoid "X times better" claims in titles unless the source states exactly that.

## 4. Sentence patterns for the narration

- **Opener (problem, agitate, way out)**: "You pick up your slides and hit record? [problem] Most viewers skip ahead after minute two. [agitate] Between the slides and the video one thing is missing, and today you build it. [way out]" Use it on the title slide or the try-it-first question.
- **Comparison (before, after, bridge)**: the left column is what learners do now, the right column is the result of doing it right, and the narration names the bridge between them (the rule, the step, the tool). Use it on `compare`.
- **Evidence (proof stack)**: one number, one sentence on what it means, one source. An odd number with a source beats a round number without one; no source, no number. Use it on `big-numbers` and `chart`.
- **Cost of doing nothing**: "Without [X], every [period] you lose [something measurable]." Once per lesson at most, in the narration.
- **Close (short AIDA)**: the key sentence, the concrete benefit, one action with a deadline, the next lesson. Never "thanks for watching".

## 5. What not to borrow from sales decks

Gradient text, glowing borders, pulsing effects (they break the three-colour rule and the no-pointless-animation rule); cards with a coloured strip on one edge (a tell-tale of machine-made slides); pricing, team and client-logo layouts; investor deck structures; charts loaded from the web (slides must work offline, so use the native `chart` type).

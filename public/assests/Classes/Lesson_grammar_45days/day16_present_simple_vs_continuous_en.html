1. Habits and routines: present simple
every day → walks
Lily walks to school every day.
💡 Every day marks a repeated routine; third-person singular Lily takes walks.
on Sundays → play
We play badminton on Sundays.
💡 On Sundays expresses repetition; we takes the base form play.

usually rides → today is walking

💡 Usually describes his habit; today marks a temporary exception.
normally eats → now is drinking
She normally eats cereal, but now she is drinking juice.
💡 Normally signals routine; now singles out the present activity.

📖 1. Detailed Rules & Spelling Patterns

1. Habits and routines: present simple

Use the present simple for repeated routines, often with every day, usually, or on Mondays.

every day → walks
Lily walks to school every day.
💡 Every day marks a repeated routine; third-person singular Lily takes walks.
on Sundays → play
We play badminton on Sundays.
💡 On Sundays expresses repetition; we takes the base form play.

2. Actions happening now: present continuous

Use am/is/are + -ing for actions happening at the moment of speaking.

right now + draw → is drawing
Lily is drawing a map right now.
💡 Right now points to an ongoing action, so is drawing fits.
at the moment + build → are building
We are building a model at the moment.
💡 At the moment identifies a current action rather than a habit.

3. Contrast a routine with a current exception

Choose the form from meaning, not simply from the subject; the same person may use both tenses.

usually rides → today is walking
Tom usually rides a bike, but today he is walking.
💡 Usually describes his habit; today marks a temporary exception.
normally eats → now is drinking
She normally eats cereal, but now she is drinking juice.
💡 Normally signals routine; now singles out the present activity.

4. Stative verbs: know, believe, understand

Verbs of knowledge and belief normally use the simple form even when the state is true now.

know → know (not am knowing)
I know the answer.
💡 Know describes a mental state, not a temporary observable action.
understand → understands
My brother understands the instructions.
💡 Understand names a state of comprehension; brother takes -s in the simple present.

5. Stative verbs: like, love, want, need, own

Preferences, needs, and possession are normally described with the present simple.

want → wants
Emma wants a new notebook.
💡 Want describes a desire, so simple present is natural.
own → owns
My uncle owns a bicycle shop.
💡 Own expresses possession, not an action in progress.
like → likes
He likes mangoes.
💡 Like names a preference; he requires likes.

6. Have: possession vs activity

Have for possession is usually simple; have in activities like having lunch can be continuous.

possess a pet → have
I have a puppy.
💡 Here have means possess, which is a state.
eat lunch now → am having
I am having lunch now.
💡 Having lunch names an activity in progress, not possession.

7. Think: opinion vs active thinking

Think meaning believe usually stays simple; think about meaning consider can be continuous.

opinion → think
I think this book is interesting.
💡 Think reports an opinion, so the simple present is natural.
consider now → am thinking
I am thinking about my science project.
💡 Thinking about describes a mental activity underway.

🎯 2. Real-World Usage Scenarios

⚠️ 3. Common Mistakes: Wrong vs Right

MISTAKE 1
❌ He is knowing the answer.
✅ He knows the answer.
💡 Know is normally stative, so use present simple.
MISTAKE 2
❌ She go to school every day.
✅ She goes to school every day.
💡 Repeated habit takes simple present; she requires goes.
MISTAKE 3
❌ Look! The baby sleeps.
✅ Look! The baby is sleeping.
💡 Look signals an action happening now.
MISTAKE 4
❌ I am having two brothers.
✅ I have two brothers.
💡 Have here describes a family relationship, not an ongoing activity.

❓ Quick FAQ

Can “always” occur with the continuous?

Yes, sometimes to emphasize a repeated behavior, often with annoyance: “He is always losing his keys.”

Does “now” always require -ing?

No. Stative verbs can still be simple: “I understand now.”

Is “I am loving it” always wrong?

Not always; it can be used informally for a temporary strong feeling. For A1 grammar, “I love it” is the usual pattern.
